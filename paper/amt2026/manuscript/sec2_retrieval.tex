\section{Retrieval formulation}
\label{sec:2}

\subsection{Forward model in the extinction domain}
\label{sec:2.1}

The cavity output is converted to an extinction coefficient before any spectral fitting
is attempted. For a sample spectrum $I(\lambda)$ and a reference spectrum
$I_0(\lambda)$ recorded with the cavity filled by zero air,
\begin{equation}
\alpha(\lambda) \;=\;
\Bigl[\underbrace{\tfrac{R_L\,(1-R(\lambda,t))}{d}}_{\textstyle \alpha_{\mathrm{mirror}}}
\;+\; R_L\,\alpha_{\mathrm{Ray}}(\lambda; T_0, P_0)\Bigr]
\frac{I_0(\lambda)-I(\lambda)}{I(\lambda)}
\;-\;\bigl[\alpha_{\mathrm{Ray}}(\lambda; T, P)-\alpha_{\mathrm{Ray}}(\lambda; T_0,P_0)\bigr],
\label{eq:1}
\end{equation}
where $R(\lambda,t)$ is the mirror reflectivity, $d$ the mirror separation, $R_L$ the
ratio of the full mirror separation to the effective cavity length actually filled by
the sample (i.e. excluding the purge-gas volumes at the mirrors), and
$\alpha_{\mathrm{Ray}}$ the Rayleigh extinction evaluated at the temperature and
pressure of the sample $(T,P)$ and of the reference $(T_0,P_0)$. The second bracket
removes the Rayleigh difference between the two states; the first converts a relative
intensity change into an absolute extinction.

Working in $\alpha$ rather than in optical density has one consequence that organises
everything below: \strong{the gas amounts enter Eq.~(\ref{eq:1}) linearly.} The quantity to be fitted
is a sum of reference cross sections with concentration coefficients, plus a smooth
background, plus whatever periodic structure the optics impose. No logarithm stands
between the unknowns and the measurement.

\subsection{Separable structure}
\label{sec:2.2}

Let $p$ index the $n$ detector pixels of the fitting window and let
\begin{equation*}
x(p) \;=\; \frac{2\,(p-p_{\min})}{p_{\max}-p_{\min}} - 1 \;\in\; [-1,1].
\end{equation*}
The model for the measured extinction is
\begin{equation}
\alpha_{\text{model}}(p)\;=\;
\sum_{i=1}^{m} c_i\,\tau_i(T)\,\sigma_i\bigl(p_i'\bigr)
\;+\;\sum_{j=0}^{q} b_j\,T_j\bigl(x(p)\bigr)
\;+\; a_s \sin(f p) + a_c \cos(f p),
\label{eq:2}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
p_i' \;=\; (p-p_c)\,s_i + p_c + \delta_i ,
\label{eq:3}
\end{equation}
with $T_j$ the Chebyshev polynomial of the first kind, $p_c$ a fixed window centre,
and $\tau_i(T)=1+\kappa_i (T-T_{\mathrm{ref}})/100$ the temperature scaling of the
$i$-th cross section. Each reference carries its own wavelength shift $\delta_i$
(pixels) and squeeze $s_i$; in practice one gas is designated the anchor and the
companions are linked to it or fixed, which is a constraint on $\theta$ and not a
change of structure.

Two choices in Eq.~(\ref{eq:2}) deserve comment.

\emph{The background is Chebyshev, not monomial.} On $x\in[-1,1]$ the Chebyshev basis is
far better conditioned than $1,p,p^2,\dots$; with the monomial basis the polynomial
block alone reaches condition numbers that make the background coefficients
meaningless, and the ill-conditioning leaks into the gas coefficients through the
shared normal equations.

\emph{The etalon enters as a sine--cosine pair at fixed frequency, not as an
amplitude--phase pair.} Since
\begin{equation*}
A\sin(fp+\varphi)=A\cos\varphi\,\sin(fp)+A\sin\varphi\,\cos(fp),
\end{equation*}
the two
parametrisations span the same model space, but the pair $(a_s,a_c)$ is \strong{linear}
whereas $(A,\varphi)$ is not. Writing it this way removes a nonlinear parameter from
the search and makes the etalon amplitude and phase part of the globally solvable
linear step. Only the frequency $f$ remains fixed a priori.

Collecting the $k=m+(q+1)+2$ basis vectors into a design matrix $\mathbf{A}(\theta)$
whose columns depend on the nonlinear vector $\theta=(\delta_i,s_i)$ only through
Eq.~(\ref{eq:3}), and writing $\mathbf{W}=\mathrm{diag}(w_1,\dots,w_n)$ for the pixel weights,
the problem is
\begin{equation}
\min_{\theta,\;\mathbf{c}}\;
\bigl\|\mathbf{W}\bigl(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{A}(\theta)\,\mathbf{c}\bigr)\bigr\|_2^2,
\qquad \mathbf{y}=\alpha_{\text{meas}} .
\label{eq:4}
\end{equation}
This is a \strong{separable} nonlinear least-squares problem in the sense of \citet{Golub_1973}:
$k$ linear parameters and, for the operational configuration, two to
six nonlinear ones.

\subsection{Variable projection with an analytic projected Jacobian}
\label{sec:2.3}

For any $\theta$ the inner problem is an ordinary linear least-squares problem, so
$\mathbf{c}$ need never be searched. Substituting the minimiser
$\hat{\mathbf{c}}(\theta)=\mathbf{A}^{+}\mathbf{y}$ leaves the \emph{projected} residual
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{r}(\theta)\;=\;\bigl(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{A}(\theta)\mathbf{A}(\theta)^{+}\bigr)\mathbf{y}
\;=\;\mathbf{P}^{\perp}_{\mathbf{A}(\theta)}\,\mathbf{y},
\label{eq:5}
\end{equation}
a function of $\theta$ alone. We minimise Eq.~(\ref{eq:5}) with a bounded
Levenberg--Marquardt/trust-region solver. In implementation the weighted, column-scaled
design matrix is factorised as $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{S}^{-1}=\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{R}$, which
delivers $\hat{\mathbf{c}}$, the residual, and the projector
$\mathbf{P}^{\perp}=\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{Q}^{\!\top}$ from one factorisation.

The Jacobian of Eq.~(\ref{eq:5}) is supplied analytically in the full Golub--Pereyra form,
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial \mathbf{r}}{\partial \theta_k}
\;=\;-\Bigl[\;\underbrace{\mathbf{P}^{\perp}\mathbf{D}_k\,\hat{\mathbf{c}}}_{\text{projection term}}
\;+\;\underbrace{(\mathbf{A}^{+})^{\!\top}\mathbf{D}_k^{\top}\,\mathbf{r}}_{\text{second term}}\;\Bigr],
\qquad
\mathbf{D}_k=\frac{\partial \mathbf{A}}{\partial \theta_k},
\label{eq:6}
\end{equation}
with $(\mathbf{A}^{+})^{\!\top}=\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{R}^{-\top}$. Only the gas columns of
$\mathbf{D}_k$ are non-zero: differentiating Eq.~(\ref{eq:3}),
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial}{\partial \delta_i}\,\sigma_i(p_i') = \sigma_i'(p_i'),
\qquad
\frac{\partial}{\partial s_i}\,\sigma_i(p_i') = (p-p_c)\,\sigma_i'(p_i'),
\label{eq:7}
\end{equation}
so $\sigma_i'$ is obtained once from the derivative of the interpolating spline that
already represents each reference. The polynomial and etalon columns are independent of
$\theta$ and contribute nothing. Both terms of Eq.~(\ref{eq:6}) cost one triangular solve and
one multiplication by $\mathbf{Q}$; the derivation and the invariance of Eq.~(\ref{eq:6}) under
the column scaling $\mathbf{S}$ are given in Appendix~\ref{app:B}.

Retaining the second term matters for two reasons. It is what makes Eq.~(\ref{eq:6}) the exact
derivative rather than the \citet{Kaufman_1975} approximation, and -- because it is exact -- it
can be checked against finite differences to the level of round-off, which turns the
Jacobian into a regression test rather than a claim (Sect.~\ref{sec:3.2}).

\strong{Relation to existing DOAS implementations.} The separable structure is not new to
DOAS; what is absent from the established treatments is the derivative of the linear
solution with respect to the nonlinear parameters. QDOAS solves the slant columns and
the polynomial by singular value decomposition inside each evaluation of the fitting
function while its Marquardt--Levenberg loop iterates over the nonlinear parameters
only -- the structure WinDOAS itself named ``Marquardt-SVD'' -- but its source states that
the derivatives with respect to shift and stretch are ``always numeric'', so no term of
the form of Eq.~(\ref{eq:6}) is formed. DOASIS \citep{Kraus_2006} goes the other way: once shift and
squeeze are fitted, the concentrations are handed to the nonlinear solver as well, and
the thesis notes that the algorithm may then fail to find the global optimum that the
linear step would have guaranteed. The standard textbook scheme \citep[][Sect.~8.3.4]{Platt} alternates one linear solve with one Levenberg--Marquardt step, explicitly ``with
fixed parameters $a_j$ and $c_h$'' -- block-coordinate optimisation, in which
$\partial \hat{\mathbf{c}}/\partial\theta$ does not appear. Outside DOAS, the analytic
form of Eq.~(\ref{eq:6}) is established in atmospheric remote sensing: \citet{B_rligea_2023}
use exactly this projected derivative, with the Kaufman variant, for a SWIR retrieval
in which -- as in BIRRA \citep{Gimeno_Garc_a_2011} -- the separability is inverted
relative to Eq.~(\ref{eq:2}).

\subsection{Time-resolved mirror reflectivity}
\label{sec:2.4}

$R$ in Eq.~(\ref{eq:1}) is not a constant of the deployment. It is determined at intervals from
helium injections and drifts between them. Rather than assigning one value per
campaign, or per day, we carry $R(\lambda,t)$ as a monotone piecewise-cubic (PCHIP)
interpolation through the injection knots, evaluated at the timestamp of each spectrum.
Monotone interpolation is chosen so that the reconstruction cannot overshoot between
knots; the cost, quantified in Sect.~\ref{sec:4.4}, is that it also cannot represent a turning point
that falls between two knots.

\subsection{Uncertainty}
\label{sec:2.5}

With $\hat{\sigma}^2 = \mathbf{r}^{\!\top}\mathbf{r}/(n-P)$, where $P$ counts the
linear columns and the active nonlinear parameters, the conditional covariance of the
linear coefficients is the usual
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Cov}\bigl(\mathbf{c}\,\big|\,\theta=\hat\theta\bigr)
=\hat{\sigma}^2\bigl(\mathbf{A}_{\mathbf{W}}^{\top}\mathbf{A}_{\mathbf{W}}\bigr)^{-1}.
\label{eq:8}
\end{equation}
Equation~(\ref{eq:8}) is what DOAS retrievals conventionally report, and it is conditional: it
holds the fitted shift and squeeze at their optimum. Variable projection separates
$\theta$ from $\mathbf{c}$ in the \emph{solution}, but it does not separate them in the
\emph{uncertainty} -- if the shift is uncertain, the concentrations inherit that uncertainty.
We therefore also form the joint covariance from the full Jacobian of the unprofiled
residual,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{J}=\bigl[\;\mathbf{A}_{\mathbf{W}}\;\big|\;\mathbf{V}\;\bigr],
\qquad
\mathbf{V}_{:,k}=\sum_i \frac{\partial \mathbf{A}_{:,i}}{\partial \theta_k}\,\hat{c}_i,
\qquad
\mathrm{Cov}(\mathbf{c},\theta)=\hat{\sigma}^2\,(\mathbf{J}^{\top}\mathbf{J})^{-1},
\label{eq:9}
\end{equation}
and take the gas block \citep{OLeary_2013}. Because Eq.~(\ref{eq:8}) is the Schur
complement of the gas block of Eq.~(\ref{eq:9}), the joint standard errors are never smaller
than the conditional ones. Note that $\mathbf{V}$ holds $\mathbf{c}$ fixed and is
therefore \emph{not} the projected Jacobian of Eq.~(\ref{eq:6}); using Eq.~(\ref{eq:6}) here would count the
linear block twice. Both quantities are reported, never one overwriting the other, so
that the size of the difference can be read directly (Appendix~\ref{app:B4} gives it for a small test problem).

When $n-P\le 0$ the variance estimate is not defined and is reported as missing rather
than as a finite number; the scan is flagged as underdetermined and retained.

\subsection{Termination state}
\label{sec:2.6}

A fit that stops because it hit a parameter bound, and a fit that stops because the
gradient vanished, are not the same result, but a residual-and-$\chi^2$ report cannot
tell them apart. We therefore classify and export the termination state of the
nonlinear stage separately from the solver's own success flag: CONVERGED, AT\_BOUND
(a nonlinear parameter rests on its box constraint at exit), STEP\_LIMITED (the
trust-region step was capped), MAX\_NFEV, and FAILED. The classifier is deliberately
conservative by construction: a nonlinear parameter within a small tolerance of its box
constraint (relative to the box width) is flagged even if the optimum happens to lie there,
and across the solves of one record the least favourable state is kept. It changes no
retrieved value. Its effect is on what the
record admits, and that effect is large (Sects.~\ref{sec:5.2} and \ref{sec:7.3}).

\subsection{Implementation note: scaling of the fitted quantity}
\label{sec:2.7}

Equation~(\ref{eq:4}) is solved on $\alpha$ scaled to order unity, and the linear coefficients
are scaled back afterwards. This is not cosmetic. Ambient $\alpha$ in these windows is
of order $10^{-7}\,\mathrm{cm^{-1}}$, so residuals and gradients are of order
$10^{-15}$, while the step and gradient tolerances of a general-purpose least-squares
routine default to $10^{-8}$ and are applied as absolute thresholds. The convergence
test is then satisfied at the starting point: the solver returns after a single
function evaluation, having moved the shift by nothing, and reports success. In one
operational spectrum the unscaled fit exited with shift $+0.0000$\,px after
$n_{\mathrm{fev}}=1$, while the same spectrum scaled by $10^{7}$ converged to
$-0.5000$\,px after $n_{\mathrm{fev}}=5$. We note this because it is silent -- the
failure mode produces a plausible number with a success flag -- and because it is
specific to re-implementations in scaled, tolerance-driven optimisers rather than to
the DOAS method itself.

\subsection{Constraints and options not used operationally}
\label{sec:2.8}

For completeness: the linear step can be solved with a non-negativity constraint on
the gas coefficients, and a Tikhonov penalty can be applied to the gas, etalon and
auxiliary columns (never to the Chebyshev background, which would distort it). Neither
is used in the configuration reported here -- the operational $\lambda$ is zero and the
concentrations are left unconstrained so that values near zero remain unbiased. The
analytic Jacobian of Eq.~(\ref{eq:6}) requires the unconstrained linear step: at an active
non-negativity bound the projector is not differentiable, and the implementation falls
back to finite differences in that case. Robust (Tukey bisquare) iteratively reweighted
fitting is available and likewise unused here.

\subsection{What the implementation exposes}
\label{sec:2.9}

The formulation of Sects.~\ref{sec:2.1}--\ref{sec:2.8} is not unusual, and Sect.~\ref{sec:3.3} shows that a conventional
implementation solving the same problem returns the same concentrations. What
differs is what can be asked of the retrieval afterwards, and the difference
is one of cost rather than of possibility.

Two questions are asked repeatedly in this paper. The first is what happens to
the product when a calibration reference is changed: the structural budget of
Sect.~\ref{sec:4} removes one zero-air knot, one reflectivity knot or one etalon component at
a time and re-runs the retrieval over \fieldnum{8847} product records, once per knot and
then for the pairwise and triple combinations. The second is what the
objective looks like away from the converged point: the identifiability map of
Sect.~\ref{sec:5} evaluates the projected objective on a grid of 160 points in the wavelength
shift for each of twelve spectra, and repeats a coarser version over 165.
Neither question is answered by a fit report; both are answered by re-entering
the retrieval with one input altered.

Augur is arranged so that both are loops rather than reprocessing campaigns.
The time-resolved references enter the forward model as data -- an array of
knots with times, interpolated at evaluation -- rather than as a configured
input file, so substituting one is an assignment. The projected objective is a
callable of the nonlinear parameters, separate from the optimiser that
searches it, so evaluating it at a chosen shift does not require converging a
fit. In an implementation where the reference is a file named in a
configuration and the objective is reachable only through the optimiser, the
same two questions cost one reprocessing of the campaign per answer: several
thousand for the budget, one hundred and sixty per spectrum for the map.

This is the sense in which the contribution of the software is architectural.
It is not that the quantities in Sects.~\ref{sec:4} and \ref{sec:5} cannot be obtained elsewhere. It is
that obtaining them has to be cheap enough to do by default, because the
finding of Sect.~\ref{sec:5.4} -- a nine-hour error in a reference time base, invisible in
the residual -- was not found by looking for it.
