\section{What should be reported, and what should be changed}
\label{sec:6}

This section states what a DOAS product should carry instead of a single uncertainty, and what in the measurement
schedule is worth changing. The knot gate (Sect.~\ref{sec:6.3}) and the coverage fields (Sect.~\ref{sec:6.4}) are
implemented in Augur and evaluated against the campaign. In the current version of Augur, later than the v0.2.0 used
for the results here, all three numbers of Sect.~\ref{sec:6.1} are implemented.
The fit uncertainty is exported with its residual-correlation (sandwich) counterpart (Sect.~\ref{sec:3.4}). The
structural term is computed in post-processing by re-fitting each record under the perturbations of
Sect.~\ref{sec:4.2}, rebuilt from the exported record and the stored calibration knots; on one day of the budget
window this reproduces the zero-air and reflectivity terms record by record. The context flag carries the termination
state, the distance to the nearest zero-air and reflectivity knot, the bracketing interval, an in-range flag and the
reference identity; the remaining fields of Table~\ref{tab:context_fields} are not yet exported.
Sect.~\ref{sec:6.5} names the errors that none of these measures reaches.

\subsection{Three numbers per retrieval, not one}
\label{sec:6.1}

Every retrieved amount should be reported with three numbers:
\begin{enumerate}
\item the fit uncertainty, from the covariance of the projected problem (Sect.~\ref{sec:2.5}), which describes what
   the residual supports.
\item the structural term, evaluated for that scan's own calibration context rather than as a campaign constant
   (Sect.~\ref{sec:4}).
\item a context flag recording the state of the references the scan depends on.
\end{enumerate}
The fit uncertainty is a statement about the residual of a model assumed correct, not about the inputs to that model,
and keeping the three separate preserves that distinction for whoever uses the product. Reporting per-record uncertainty
components with different correlation scales (random, structured, common) is established practice for Earth-observation
climate data records \citep{Merchant_2017}, building on \citet{JCGM_2008}; the three-number convention applies it to a single
retrieval. The fit uncertainty should
travel with the termination state of the nonlinear stage (Sect.~\ref{sec:2.6}), and, where a nonlinear parameter may be
unidentifiable, with the ratio of the objective at the returned value to that at a fixed reference point
(Sect.~\ref{sec:5.1}). The value of the context flag lies in which fields it carries, so we specify them per record and
per reference type (zero air, reflectivity) in Table~\ref{tab:context_fields}.

The last two fields address the failure of Sect.~\ref{sec:5.4}. No provenance field detects the clock error of the
raw record itself (Sect.~\ref{sec:5.4}). The fields catch the failure that followed: a reflectivity reference built before the
correction and never rebuilt. Any correction applied upstream invalidates every derived product built before it, and
a pipeline whose derived products do not record their build convention cannot tell that this has happened.

The mismatch was visible in the data as a nine-hour run of out-of-range records at the start of the deployment -- the
first zero-air block at 02:42, the first reflectivity knot at 11:40 -- with \fieldnum{42.3}\,\% of that day extrapolated. The
distance and in-range fields would have carried that anomaly into the product on the first run after the correction,
rather than two months later. Identifying its cause needed the build time of the reflectivity file and the convention
it was built under, so detection and diagnosis need separate fields. The same tags allow a load-time check that refuses a reference whose time-convention tag differs from that of the
spectra; we recommend it where the tags exist, and the report in every case so that the error stays interpretable.

The structural term cannot be obtained in closed form. The amplification factor $I_0/(I_0-I)$, which governs how an
error in the zero-air reference enters the extinction, varies by a factor of forty between records and is available
without fitting. Regressed against the measured response, however, it explains between \fieldnum{0.0}\,\% and \fieldnum{9}\,\% of the
variance, with the wrong sign. The polynomial background absorbs the smooth part of the perturbation, so what reaches
the gas columns is set by shape rather than amplitude. Normalising the response by the retrieved amount does not help: the
robust coefficient of variation is \fieldnum{0.80}--\fieldnum{1.09} in relative terms against \fieldnum{0.83}--\fieldnum{1.11} in absolute. The structural
term therefore has to be obtained by running the perturbation, which is available in post-processing but not in a
real-time monitor, and the two implementations differ accordingly.

\subsubsection{Which remedy a term needs is readable from its shape}
\label{sec:6.1a}

The shape statistic $s$ of Sect.~\ref{sec:4.3} indicates which of three actions a structural term needs. A term with
$s \approx 1$ is Gaussian: its error is spread evenly over all records and can only be reduced by a better model. A term
with $s \approx 0.7$--1 is dominated by measurement noise on the references and responds to averaging. A term with
$s \ll 0.7$ is concentrated in a few records and needs detection rather than averaging. This prediction is checked by
ordering the \fieldnum{155} hours of the budget window by their contribution to each term's variance
(Table~\ref{tab:shape_remedy}).

The ordering by $s$ is the ordering by concentration: the two-hour interval from 23:00 UTC on 18 May to 01:00 UTC on
19 May alone holds \fieldnum{37}\,\% of the reflectivity term's variance. The reflectivity hours form far fewer contiguous runs
than the same number of hours drawn at random, so they are episodes rather than scattered outliers, and a flag can
address them. The etalon hours are indistinguishable from random, the signature of a term no flag will help. On the
robust scale the ordering of the first two terms reverses (Sect.~\ref{sec:4.4}), which is why Table~\ref{tab:2} reports both scales. A term that is small for a typical record
and largest in total is the one a campaign-mean uncertainty hides.

\subsection{Calibration blocks: interval length and scans per block}
\label{sec:6.2}

The natural response to an interpolation error is to interpolate over shorter
intervals. How much that buys depends on the channel. Tripling the reflectivity interval from 2 to 6\,h was
measured to increase the interpolation error from \fieldnum{0.87}\,\% to \fieldnum{1.00}\,\% in hot PNs and from \fieldnum{2.05}\,\% to
\fieldnum{2.73}\,\% in hot ANs. In the 180\,$^{\circ}$C channel the error is set mostly by the noise on the knots, and a
shorter interval would buy little; in the 300\,$^{\circ}$C channel the interval carries curvature (Table~\ref{tab:splithalf_loo}),
and shorter intervals would help.

The other lever is the number of scans averaged into each calibration block (Appendix~\ref{app:C}). Within the operational block ($N = 34$ scans, $T = 32$\,s), doubling the number of
scans averaged reduces the scatter of contiguous sub-blocks by \fieldnum{7}\,\% in the ANs channel, against the 29\,\% that
independent scans would give. The measurable range lies below the operational block length, so the evidence is not decisive;
operating one longer block would settle it. Even with white averaging the return would be modest. The zero-air term carries
\fieldnum{45}\,\% of the $\Sigma$ANs variance, so a fourfold block, which would raise the calibration duty from 1.20 to 3.89\,\%,
would lower the structural budget from \fieldnum{0.1236} to \fieldnum{0.1008}\,ppb (\fieldnum{18.5}\,\%). Flagging the few defective
reflectivity intervals would lower it to \fieldnum{0.0901}\,ppb. That ordering -- flagging before averaging --
holds whatever the block scaling turns out to be, because the reflectivity term is concentrated in a few episodes and
the zero-air term is not (Sect.~\ref{sec:6.1a}).

\subsection{Gating defective knots}
\label{sec:6.3}

A few calibration blocks carry most of the zero-air term. Ordering the cold-channel zero-air blocks by their
leave-one-out influence, the worst single block carries \fieldnum{28}\,\% of the total variance, the worst three \fieldnum{76}\,\% and the worst
five \fieldnum{97}\,\%, so a structural term computed over all blocks is a statement about a handful of them. They are identifiable
before any fit is attempted. The block's light level relative to the campaign median, with the threshold
$I/\tilde{I} < 0.5$, selects only \fieldnum{13} of the \fieldnum{38} blocks whose influence exceeds 2\,\%. Those \fieldnum{13}, however, include nine of
the worst ten and account for \fieldnum{99.9}\,\% of the influence variance; the \fieldnum{25} blocks it misses are all in the
\fieldnum{2}--\fieldnum{3}\,\% range, and the threshold excludes \fieldnum{8}\,\% of blocks. Classification accuracy would have rejected this threshold, but the relevant
figure of merit is the variance removed, and a single observable removes essentially all of it.

Only the zero-air reference needed this gate. Reflectivity knots already pass three quality gates: a minimum fraction
of valid pixels, an acceptance band on the retrieved effective path length, and limits on the calibration residual.
A defective helium or zero-air pair is therefore rejected before it becomes a knot, whereas every zero-air block became
a knot regardless of how much light reached the detector. The gate is implemented with the threshold expressed as a fraction
of the run's own median block intensity rather than as an absolute count, so it transfers between instruments, and it
is disabled by default so that reprocessing an existing product is an explicit choice.

\subsection{Coverage: say when the reference is far away}
\label{sec:6.4}

The coverage deficits of Table~\ref{tab:coverage} have two causes that cost different things to remove. Extrapolation
(records outside the knot range) and gaps (records between knots more than two hours apart) both concern the
reflectivity, since zero air has neither. The first cause is bookkeeping and the cheapest fix in this paper:
reflectivity generation stopped at 07-09, which produces the hot channel's trailing extrapolation and is removed by
re-running over files that already exist. The second is the clustered rejection of cold calibration pairs described in
Sect.~\ref{sec:5.3}; its remedy is to restore the light level or, failing that, redundancy. Only this second cause
concerns how often the instrument calibrates, and even there the average rate is not the problem, because the
rejections arrive in the same episodes that carry the reflectivity variance (Table~\ref{tab:shape_remedy}).

Quality control has a coverage cost. In the cold channel the reflectivity gates are, correctly, the proximate cause of
the coverage deficit. This qualifies the argument of Sect.~\ref{sec:6.3} for a zero-air gate: gates remove variance and
coverage, and a pipeline that gates without reporting the cost has moved the error somewhere harder to see. The context flag of Sect.~\ref{sec:6.1} records that cost. The same caution applies to the budget. The
reflectivity contribution of Sect.~\ref{sec:4} is measured by removing a knot and re-interpolating, so its magnitude
scales with the knot spacing, and a sparse reference appears as a dominant term partly because it is sparse. The remedy
therefore belongs to the schedule and to reprocessing rather than to the retrieval. Records in gaps, which the budget cannot cover, are reported with the context flag set and excluded from any
statistic that assumes a characterised uncertainty.

Each reflectivity knot pairs an hourly zero-air block with the most recent accepted helium block, measured every three
hours (Appendix~\ref{app:A}, Table~\ref{tab:cal_schedule}). A rejected zero-air/helium pair removes one hourly knot and leaves a two-hour gap; a rejected
helium block removes no knot but makes the following knots use helium measured up to six hours earlier. Matching the
helium cadence to the zero-air cadence would bring the total calibration duty from 1.20\,\% to 1.79\,\% and make the
helium half of every knot contemporaneous. Whether it would also shorten the gaps depends on why pairs are rejected,
which in the cold channel is mainly the light level. The case for it is redundancy against runs of rejection and
contemporaneity, not precision: in the 180\,$^{\circ}$C channel the reflectivity error depends only weakly on interval
length (Sect.~\ref{sec:6.2}). At 1.2\,\% of the measurement time, calibration is the binding constraint on none of these
remedies.

\subsection{Errors that neither the fit report nor the proposed fields detect}
\label{sec:6.5}

The convention of Sect.~\ref{sec:6.1} makes visible what the pipeline already knows. It cannot make visible what the
pipeline does not know, and two such errors appear in this record.

The first is a multiplicative gain error. Against a co-located NIER analyser the heated-channel NO$_2$ reads low by a
factor of about \fieldnum{0.6} while the ambient-temperature channel agrees (Sect.~\ref{sec:3.6}). A gain leaves the residual, the
reported uncertainty, the termination state and every distance and provenance field unchanged, and the zero-air test
of Sect.~\ref{sec:7.2} is blind to it because a gain applied to zero is zero. Only a comparison with an independent
measurement of the same quantity reveals it, and does not by itself identify the cause.

The second is the raw light level. All six ambient-temperature excursions of Sect.~\ref{sec:7.3} coincide with spectra
that fell below 30\,\% of their usual count at 460\,nm; the residual finds them, and the reference context is abnormal
in five of them, but none of the proposed fields names the cause. The light level of each spectrum, and the helium and
zero-air intensities of each calibration block rather than only their ratio, are known when they are recorded and
could travel with the product as further context; we have not evaluated them for the heated channels.
