\section{Structural error budget}
\label{sec:4}

\subsection{What the fit reports, and what it cannot report}
\label{sec:4.1}

The covariance returned by the fit (Sect.~\ref{sec:2.5}) propagates one thing: the residual
scatter of the spectrum being fitted. For the operational configuration this
gives a single-scan reported uncertainty of \fieldnum{0.55}\,\% on the channel NO$_2$
amount, and it is the number that appears in the data product.

That number is conditional on the forward model being correct. Three inputs of
Eq.~(\ref{eq:1}) are not measured simultaneously with the spectrum, but interpolated to
its time from neighbouring calibration blocks: the zero-air reference $I_0$,
the mirror reflectivity $R(\lambda,t)$, and -- for the periodic optical
structure -- the etalon frequency $f$, which is held at a fixed value rather
than redetermined per scan. An error in any of these enters $\alpha$ directly
and is therefore indistinguishable, within a single fit, from a change in the
gas amounts. The residual does not see it, so the reported uncertainty does
not contain it.

Two further inputs were tested. Sample temperature and pressure, interpolated the
same way as $I_0$, contribute below \fieldnum{0.1}\,\%. For the instrument line shape the test is weaker than
it looks. The kernel is resampled onto the detector grid with a radius of
$\lfloor 4\sigma \rfloor + 1$ pixels, and at the mismatch measured in the ambient-temperature channel
(FWHM 0.765 against 0.785\,nm) the retrieved amount moved by $\sim 10^{-5}$\,\%; this shows that the test did
not carry a width change of that size into the forward model, not that the retrieval is insensitive to it.
A test with a continuously resampled kernel has not been made, and no mismatch was measured for the heated
channels. The residual does carry a signal-proportional model error: the median reduced $\chi^2$ of the
300\,$^{\circ}$C channel rises from \fieldnum{1.05} at NO$_2$(180\,$^{\circ}$C) below 1\,ppb to \fieldnum{1.57} at 10--20\,ppb. That error is
not in the budget below; the cross sections and the line shape are candidates.

\subsection{Measuring a structural term}
\label{sec:4.2}

A structural term is measured by perturbing the input the way the operational
pipeline can actually be wrong, then re-running the full retrieval and reading
the change in the retrieved amount. Two perturbations are used.

\strong{Leave-one-out (LOO).} One calibration knot is removed, the remaining knots
are re-interpolated across the resulting gap, and every scan in that gap is
re-retrieved. This is an upper bound by construction: the interval the
interpolant must span is roughly doubled relative to operation.

\strong{Split-half.} Each calibration block is split into two halves -- contiguous (first and second
half) or interleaved (even and odd scans) -- and two independent block means are formed. Their difference measures the noise on a
knot with no change of interval, and therefore requires no extrapolation back
to the operational spacing. With $n/2$ scans per half the difference carries a
factor of two relative to the full-block knot noise, which is divided out.

The two disagree by a channel-dependent factor, and that disagreement is itself
the result (Table~\ref{tab:splithalf_loo}).

A ratio near unity means the LOO error is entirely knot noise -- the interpolant
is not being asked to bridge any real curvature, and halving the reference
interval would not improve it. A ratio near one half means the interval does
carry curvature. The ordering agrees with the direct measurement of Sect.~\ref{sec:6.2}:
tripling the reflectivity interval from 2 to 6\,h raises the interpolation error
by \fieldnum{15}\,\% in hot PNs and by \fieldnum{33}\,\% in hot ANs.

This bears on a recommendation made
qualitatively by \citet{Washenfelder_2008}. For the 180\,$^{\circ}$C channel the
interpolation error is mostly knot noise (ratio \fieldnum{0.74}--\fieldnum{0.91}): measuring the
references \emph{more often} would reduce it little, and averaging more scans per calibration block is the
lever that could. For the 300\,$^{\circ}$C channel (\fieldnum{0.40}--\fieldnum{0.43}) the interval carries curvature, and
shorter intervals would help. Contiguous and interleaved halves give similar knot noise in the NO$_2$
amount (the contiguous estimate is \fieldnum{7}--\fieldnum{19}\,\% smaller), so the interleaved split does not understate it.

\subsection{Terms must be propagated jointly, and the scale taken once}
\label{sec:4.3}

It is standard to measure each term separately and add the results in
quadrature. Here that is wrong twice over, and the two failures have different
causes.

\strong{Model-space and $\alpha$-space perturbations are not additive.} Perturbing
$I_0$ and perturbing $R$ both move $\alpha$, and their effects add scan by
scan: the median of $|\,\delta_{\mathrm{joint}} - (\delta_{I_0} +
\delta_{R})\,|$ is \fieldnum{0.8}--\fieldnum{2.2}\,\% of the signal. The etalon is different. Its sine
and cosine columns are free linear parameters, so they absorb part of any
smooth perturbation of $\alpha$ before it can reach the gas columns. Perturbing
all three simultaneously gives a smaller result than summing the three
responses, by \fieldnum{8.3}\,\% (hot ANs), \fieldnum{6.4}\,\% (hot PNs) and \fieldnum{$-0.2$}\,\% (cold). Adding the
etalon term as if it were independent therefore overestimates, and by an amount
that depends on the fitting window and polynomial order.

\strong{Neither variances nor robust scales add.} Summarising each term by a
median-based scale and combining those in quadrature misses the directly
measured joint scale by \fieldnum{$+13.3$}\,\% (hot ANs), \fieldnum{$-8.1$}\,\% (hot PNs) and \fieldnum{$+6.9$}\,\%
($\Sigma$ANs). Performing the identical combination on standard deviations
does not repair it: the joint exceeds the quadrature sum by \fieldnum{15.5}\,\% for the ANs
channel and \fieldnum{7.6}\,\% for $\Sigma$ANs, and falls \fieldnum{2.5}\,\% below it for PNs. Neither space is additive, and neither
discrepancy has a predictable sign.

For variances the failure has two measured parts. The single-term responses of $\Sigma$ANs are correlated
(pairwise $r = \fieldnum{0.25}$, \fieldnum{0.18} and \fieldnum{0.03}), which adds \fieldnum{28.7}\,\% to the sum of their variances, and the terms
do not superpose -- the etalon interaction above -- which removes \fieldnum{12.9}\,\%, leaving the joint variance
\fieldnum{15.8}\,\% above the sum. Robust scales fail in addition through distribution shape. Write
$s$ for the normalised shape statistic $1.4826\,\mathrm{MAD}/\mathrm{SD}$,
which is unity for a Gaussian and 0.727 for a Laplace. The three $\Sigma$ANs
components have $s = \fieldnum{0.73}$ ($I_0$), \fieldnum{0.32} ($R$) and \fieldnum{0.98} (etalon): one is
Gaussian, one Laplace-like, one very much heavier-tailed. A median-based
scale therefore captures a different fraction of the variance in each, and the
joint series has a fourth shape again ($s = \fieldnum{0.56}$). A variance is indifferent
to shape, so for variances the discrepancy is covariance and interaction alone.

What makes this fatal for quadrature is not the size of the error but its
unpredictability. The discrepancy is \fieldnum{$+13$}\,\% in one channel and \fieldnum{$-8$}\,\% in
another, and moving to variances neither removes it nor changes its sign --
\fieldnum{$+15.5$}\,\%, \fieldnum{$-2.5$}\,\% and \fieldnum{$+7.6$}\,\% for the same three series -- so it cannot be
absorbed into a correction factor: knowing the factor
requires knowing the covariance, interaction and shape of the joint response, and that is available
only after the joint propagation has been performed. Quadrature of robust
scales cannot be repaired, only replaced. The rule used throughout this paper
is therefore:
\begin{quote}
Propagate every term, simultaneously, into the final product time series.
Take the scale once, at the end, with the same estimator used for the
quantity it will be compared against.
\end{quote}

Records whose perturbed fit terminated at a parameter bound are excluded, for
consistency with Sect.~\ref{sec:5.2}: a fit pinned at a bound has not converged, and its
response is not a measurement of anything.

The cold channel is not included in Table~\ref{tab:2}. It was not re-run on the
all-record basis, which lies outside the critical path of this paper, and its
earlier representative-record values are withdrawn rather than reported on a
different basis.

The record evaluated here is the product reprocessed on the clock-aligned
reflectivity reference (Sect.~\ref{sec:5.4}). It is not the dataset delivered to the
campaign, which was produced before that correction and is retained as
delivered.

Table~\ref{tab:2} is conditional on a population. A perturbation experiment can only be
run on scans that have neighbouring knots of both reference types, so periods
in which the calibration itself failed are absent from it -- and those are
precisely the periods with the largest errors. How far each record sits from
its nearest knot of each type, how often the interpolation is in fact an
extrapolation, and which periods those are, is measured campaign-wide in Sect.~\ref{sec:5.3}.
The budget window of this section is the best-covered stretch of the
deployment: no record outside the knot range and no gap longer than two hours,
against \fieldnum{11.03}\,\% of cold records in gaps longer than two hours over the campaign
as a whole. Table~\ref{tab:2} therefore describes the instrument when its calibration is
working, and Sect.~\ref{sec:5.3} measures how often that condition does not hold.

Against what the fit reports the budget is large. A budget record is one 60\,s
ambient bin, which at the operational scan rate is one scan, and the median
fit-propagated $\Sigma$ANs uncertainty at that resolution, over the same records, is \fieldnum{0.0541}\,ppb. The
structural contribution alone is therefore \fieldnum{1.28} times the reported sigma on
the robust scale and \fieldnum{2.29} times it in standard deviation, and the total
uncertainty is \strong{\fieldnum{1.63} to \fieldnum{2.50} times what the fit reports}, the range spanning
the two scales rather than any doubt about the measurement. Because leave-one-out doubles the
interpolation interval (Sect.~\ref{sec:4.2}) and the window is the best-calibrated week of the deployment,
these are upper-bound estimates for a well-calibrated period.

The denominator has to be argued for, because three other quantities are
available and none of them answers this question (Table~\ref{tab:denominators}).

The column shipped with the product is not a measurement uncertainty. It is a

(Sect.~\ref{sec:3.6}). Comparing the

it asks whether structure is comparable to the gain term, not whether it
exceeds the precision the retrieval claims. The 5\,min row is the fit
uncertainty averaged over five scans -- over the deployment it is $\fieldnum{0.0558}/\sqrt{5}$ -- and dividing it into a structural scale measured at 60\,s would mix

lies between \fieldnum{0.0217} and \fieldnum{0.0326}\,ppb (5th and 95th percentiles).

\subsection{Consistency with the observed variation}
\label{sec:4.5}

An error budget assembled from perturbation experiments can be checked against
the data it claims to describe. If a term is channel-differential, it must
appear in the channel-difference product; if it varies on the timescale of the
calibration interval, it must appear in the hour-to-hour variation of that
product. The observed variation is an upper bound on the measurement error,
because it also contains real atmospheric variability.

The comparison is only meaningful if both sides pass through the same
estimator. The observed quantity is summarised by
$1.4826\,\mathrm{median}|\Delta|/\sqrt{2}$ over adjacent hourly means, so the
propagated series is summarised the same way.

The budget series is itself hourly (median spacing between consecutive entries
\fieldnum{1.00}\,h, $p_{90}$ \fieldnum{1.03}\,h), so the two series describe the same lag, and the
comparison is made on the same timestamps, paired.

Two comparisons are defensible, and they differ because the perturbation
series and the observed series have opposite temporal correlation. Their
measured lag-1 autocorrelations are \fieldnum{$-0.60$} for the budget and
\fieldnum{$+0.73$} for the observation in the matched hours. The negative value is a property of
the experiment, not of the instrument: removing a knot pins the interpolant at
the two neighbours and forces it to depart on alternate sides, so the
perturbation response alternates in sign from hour to hour. The positive value
is atmospheric persistence.

The comparison is made at the resolution at which the observed bound is
defined -- hourly means, differenced -- and against the same hours (Table~\ref{tab:hourly_check}).

The last row uses a different operator and is given only to show the size of
that choice; both ratios in the column are taken against the same matched
denominator, \fieldnum{0.082}\,ppb. Both the numerator and the denominator of the ratio quoted here
are evaluated on the same \fieldnum{154} matched hours and \fieldnum{153} consecutive pairs; the
campaign-wide denominator gives almost the same ratio, \fieldnum{0.76}.

The budget predicts \strong{\fieldnum{0.75} times} the observed hour-to-hour variation (Fig.~\ref{fig:4}b). It
does not exceed it, as it must not, and it is not far below it either, which
would have implied that the observed variation is dominated by something the
budget does not describe.

We report the two as consistent, and assign no pass or fail. The test cannot do better: it assumes the error is white at one-hour lag
(any slower structural component is invisible to it), it cannot separate
measurement error from atmospheric variability, and the upper bound doubles
the interpolation interval by construction. The choice of operator alone moves the ratio from \fieldnum{0.75}
to \fieldnum{0.84} (Table~\ref{tab:hourly_check}); differences of that size are the resolution of the method.

One correction must be avoided, because it is available and wrong. Since
$\operatorname{Var}(\Delta) = 2\sigma^2(1-\rho)$ is an identity when $\rho$ is
the lag-1 autocorrelation of the same series, ``correcting'' the observed
increment statistic for $\rho$ returns the standard deviation of the observed
series itself -- \fieldnum{0.366}\,ppb for the hourly $\Sigma$ANs, i.e. the full
geophysical variability. Comparing a structural budget against that number is
not a test; the differencing was introduced precisely to remove it.

\subsection{What should be reported instead}
\label{sec:4.6}

For each retrieved amount the product should carry three numbers, not one: the
fit-reported uncertainty; the structural contribution evaluated for that scan's
calibration context; and a flag identifying scans whose calibration knots are
themselves suspect. The third matters because the structural error is not
uniform -- the $R$ term carries the smallest shape statistic in Table~\ref{tab:2}
($s = \fieldnum{0.32}$ for $\Sigma$ANs), meaning a small number of intervals carry most of
its variance. Section~\ref{sec:6} specifies all three and states which are implemented. Sections~\ref{sec:5.3} and \ref{sec:5.4} add two
further fields of the same kind -- how far the record is from its calibration,
and whether the reference it used was still valid -- which the pipeline can
supply without any new measurement. A further step, not attempted here, would treat the zero-air reference and the
reflectivity as measured quantities with their own covariance and propagate the structural term inside the fit
rather than afterwards.
